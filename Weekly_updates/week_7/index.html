The three inspection rovers, built and running in the simulator

Imported from the supplied CAD · assembled as working machines

All three rover machines — Dyna Flux, OMNI and Orbit — now exist inside the simulator as working models, built from the CAD files supplied for each machine. The picture below is the simulator's own view of them, on a 500 mm pipe.

What “built” means here

This is not a picture of a CAD file. Each machine was taken from its STEP file and turned into something the physics engine can actually drive:

  • the assembly is split into the parts that move separately — each wheel, each rocker, the probe — and each becomes its own body;
  • those bodies are joined by the joints the CAD implies: wheels turn on their axles, the Dyna Flux hinge lets its two halves pitch relative to each other, the OMNI's rear arm rocks;
  • each body carries its mass and rotational inertia, so the machine behaves like an object of that weight and shape rather than a shell;
  • the surfaces that actually touch the pipe are handled separately from the surfaces you see, so contact is computed on the real contact geometry.

What these models are used for

They are the basis of every rover measurement the project reports — magnetic hold, driving on the pipe, turning. Because each is built from the supplied CAD rather than approximated, a dimension checked in the simulator is the dimension in the drawing.

The UT probe now takes a wall-thickness reading on contact

Reads on touch — including a one-second touch · finds a corroded patch

Until now the drone could fly to the chimney and press its ultrasonic probe against it, and that was all — the simulation recorded that contact happened, but produced no measurement. The probe now takes a wall-thickness reading the moment it touches, displays it, and records it.

The reading is taken and locked in at the instant of contact, not after a hold. That was the requirement, and it is why a one-second touch reports exactly what a ten-second touch reports (proved below).

The chimney now has a wall

The 45 m chimney is divided into three thickness bands, and one patch of corrosion was placed in the top band so the inspection has something to find:

What it looks like in the cockpit view

Where the numbers come from — please read this line

The chimney model is a solid shape with no wall thickness of its own, so the three thicknesses and the corroded patch were specified for this exercise, not measured from a real stack. What has been built and proved is the instrument: the probe finds the surface, reads the wall at the exact point it touched, flags a thin one, and records it. Give it real thickness data and it reports that instead.

Orbit rover: a full lap around the pipe

4 laps out of 4 · never came off · 4.2× margin

The Orbit rover drives right around the outside of a 500 mm pipe — over the top, down one side, underneath, and back up — held on by its magnet wheels the whole way. It then advances along the pipe to the next ring.

Measured result

Two runs of two laps each. Every lap completed, and the wheels never lifted anywhere near the point that would count as coming off.

A wheel is counted as detached if it lifts more than 5 mm off the steel. The worst lift across all four laps was 1.18 mm — a margin of 4.2×. Each lap also has to end where it should: all four came in between 360.04° and 360.10°, so the rover finishes a lap within a tenth of a degree of exactly once round.

All four laps are drawn on both panels. They lie almost on top of one another, which is the point — the machine does the same thing every time. The numbers behind both panels are in the table above.

After each lap the rover moves along the pipe to start the next ring. That step was measured too: it was asked for 150 mm and delivered 150.4, 152.6, 151.1 and 151.8 mm — within 2.6 mm every time.

Scope of this result

This covers the lap around the pipe and the advance along it. Turning the wheels from along-the-pipe to around-the-pipe is a separate step that is still being worked on and is not claimed here.

Fuller detail lives in SIM_HANDBOOK.html (worlds, startup, rovers) and GEN5_MODEL_SPEC.html (drone specifications), both in this same folder.

The flight that failed, and why it is in this report

The first attempt at the clean top wall never reached the chimney. That approach is on the far side of the stack, and the aircraft was sent there in a straight line — a line that passes straight through the chimney. It flew into the stack at 35 m, the propeller-strike crash detection reported last week fired exactly as designed, and the aircraft came down.

That was a routing fault, not a fault in the probe. The flight now goes around the stack at a constant distance — nine short steps — and the arm was re-flown clean against the same untouched prediction. The failed run is kept, unedited, with a note explaining it.

Standing method

  • Predict, then measure. The expected result is written down and checksummed before the run, and reproduced afterwards unedited, misses included.
  • Nothing validated was modified. The shipping chimney mission is checksum-gated by the regression suite; the new work is in new files and that checksum is unchanged. The rig worlds were not edited either — the display world for the photographs is a new file.
  • Failed runs are kept, not deleted, with a note saying what went wrong.

The complete record, entry by entry, is the project sync log.
