The verdict in three lines

1. The heading signal we steer on has no sign.

The value the Teensy sends to the Raspberry Pi reads exactly the same whether the rover has drifted 2° to the left or 2° to the right. The steering loop physically cannot know which way to correct. This is a property of the sensor register we chose, proven from the Murata datasheet formula and verified numerically to nine decimal places. CERTAIN

2. We throw away a free, perfect reference once per pass.

On a vertical wall, gravity is an absolute heading reference — drift-free, immune to steel, and needing no GPS. Our code captures the setpoint as target_heading = x_angle at the instant the operator pushes the stick, so the rover holds whatever angle it happened to be at, re-datumed every single pass. Each stripe is individually straight and collectively a fan. CERTAIN

3. Pixhawk / Cube Orange cannot be used here at all.

ArduPilot defines “heading” as rotation about the Earth’s vertical axis. When our rover climbs, its forward axis is that vertical axis — a mathematical singularity. The thing we actually need to control is rotation about the wall normal, a horizontal axis, for which ArduRover has no control channel. This is before we even reach the GPS and compass problems. See Tab 3. CERTAIN

Where the error comes from

All figures are the sideways drift produced over a single 10 m vertical pass by each cause acting alone. Formula: drift = pass_length × sin(heading error).

Not on the chart, because it is not a bounded number

The unsigned-heading defect (Finding 1) does not produce a fixed error — it makes the control loop unstable in one direction. In simulation, starting 3° off vertical, the loop runs away to the plot limit instead of correcting. There is no “how many millimetres” for this one; the answer is “as far as it gets before someone stops it.” See Tab 2, Finding 1.

The physics we are not using — in plain English

Imagine holding a spirit level flat on a table. Turn it left or right and nothing on the bubble changes — gravity cannot tell you which way a flat object is pointing. That is why cars and drones need a compass or GPS.

Now stick that same level flat against a vertical wall. Rotate it a couple of degrees and the bubble moves immediately and by a lot. On a wall, gravity tells you the pointing direction directly, at the maximum sensitivity physically possible — 1 g of signal per radian of angle.

Our rover is on a vertical wall. So our inclinometer is already a perfect compass for this job: no drift, no GPS, and completely unaffected by the steel of the gas holder. We verified numerically that this reading does not change at all as the rover moves around the curved circumference of the tank — the curvature is invisible to it. CERTAIN

Formula: with f = forward (up the wall) and l = lateral (across the wall), heading error φ = atan2(al, af). Sensitivity, range and drift are all optimal. Full derivation in Tab 2.

Why it cannot work

getCalculatedAngleX() returns the SCL3300’s ANG_X register. The Murata datasheet (§2.5) defines it as: 

ANG_X = atan( acc_x / √(acc_y² + acc_z²) )

The denominator is a magnitude, so it is always positive. The register therefore reports “how far the X axis is above the horizontal plane” — a quantity that is identical whether the rover leans left or right.

Proof — computed, not argued

True heading errorWhat our code reads (ANG on the travel axis)What it should read (lateral axis)
−2.0° (drifting right)+88.0000°−2.0000°

0.0° (perfectly vertical)+90.0000°0.0000°
+1.0°+89.0000°+1.0000°

Bit-identical for ±0.5°, ±1°, ±2° and ±5°, confirmed to 1×10−9. The controller receives the same number for opposite errors and applies the same correction to both.

What that does to the machine

Two aggravating factors on top

  • Wrong sensor mode. The Arduino library defaults to Mode 4 if setMode() is never called — and we never call it. Murata states: “Inclination ranges are limited in Mode 3 and Mode 4 to maximum ±10° inclination.” Our rover sits at 90°. We are operating the part outside its specified range, in its slowest filter (10 Hz, ~16 ms of lag) instead of Mode 1’s 40 Hz.
  • The % 360 wrap hides it. The library maps negative angles to 270–360°, and the Pi then does (raw − offset) % 360. The arithmetic survives, so the CSV logs look plausible. That is why this has not been spotted by inspection.
Cost if unfixed: Control loop is unstable in one direction. No gain tuning can compensate for a missing sign.

Why it is wrong

Gravity hands us an absolute reference for free. target_heading = x_angle deletes it and replaces it with a relative one. The rover holds whatever attitude it was in at the moment the stick was pushed — and this happens again on every stripe.

Combined with Finding 3 (the 15°-wide acceptance window), each pass begins from a different, uncontrolled angle. Every stripe is individually straight. Collectively they are a fan. This is the single best explanation for “it is different every time”.

Today

target_heading = x_angle

Should be

target_heading = 0.0   # true vertical

heading_offset is then calibrated once per job against a plumb line or laser level at working temperature — never per pass.

Cost if unfixed: up to 1,305 mm per 10 m pass, and different on every pass, so it cannot be compensated downstream.

The arithmetic, with no plant model needed

With proportional control only, at steady state correction = KP × error. If a constant disturbance requires C counts/s of correction to cancel it, the loop must sit at error = C / KP forever. With KP = 25:

Correction the disturbance demandsPermanent heading errorDrift over a 10 m pass
25 counts/s1.0°175 mm
50 counts/s2.0°349 mm
120 counts/s (20% of our correction limit)4.8°837 mm

This is control theory, not tuning. Raising KP shrinks the error but never removes it, and a high KP on a latency-limited loop oscillates.

What produces a constant disturbance on our rover

  • Umbilical drag — paint hose, air line, power tether. The pull grows as the rover climbs and changes direction each time it indexes sideways. LIKELY our largest single disturbance, and we currently have no instrumentation on it at all.
  • Spray-gun reaction torque, if the gun is not on the rover centreline.
  • Left/right mismatch in wheel rolling diameter, gearbox friction, or magnet drag.
Cost if unfixed: 349 mm per 10 m pass at a 2° standing error — a consistent bow, always the same way.

The proposal is to add a Pixhawk / Cube Orange running ArduRover. Our recommendation is not to. There are four independent blockers, any one of which is fatal on its own. Three are structural — no parameter, firmware version or wiring change removes them.

Recommendation

Do not purchase the Cube Orange Plus (₹51,999). ArduRover’s navigation stack is built on an assumption our vehicle violates by construction: that the vehicle drives in the horizontal plane. Purchase one ICM-42688-P gyro (₹1,999) instead and fit it to the existing Teensy. That single chip delivers the only capability the Cube would genuinely have added.

Blocker 1 — The mathematical singularity

CERTAIN — this is the structural one.

ArduPilot defines yaw as rotation about the Earth’s vertical axis. Every Rover navigation mode — AUTO, GUIDED, STEERING, the L1/S-curve path follower, the pivot-turn logic — is built on ahrs.get_yaw().

When our rover climbs the wall, its forward axis is the Earth’s vertical axis. Pitch = 90°. At exactly 90° pitch the Euler yaw angle is mathematically undefined (gimbal lock). The internal quaternion stays valid, but the heading number that the navigation controller actually consumes becomes meaningless and flips wildly on sub-degree attitude changes.

Meanwhile the quantity we need to control — rotation about the wall normal, which is a horizontal axis in Earth frame — is not “yaw” in ArduPilot’s frame of reference at all. It is a mixture of roll and yaw for which no Rover controller has a channel.

Could we not just rotate the board?

No. Setting AHRS_ORIENTATION so the board’s Z axis lies along the wall normal makes the EKF see a permanent 1 g in body-XY. It will integrate that as a real 1 g horizontal acceleration and diverge within seconds.

Blocker 3 — No legal position source either

CERTAIN

EK3_SRC1_POSXY accepts GPS, Beacon (UWB), ExternalNav, or Optical Flow. Wheel encoders are not a position source in ArduPilot. They only supply body-frame odometry into an EKF that still needs an absolute reference and a heading. Without one of those four, EKF3 never produces a position and no autonomous mode will arm.

UWB beacons (Marvelmind) are the only non-GPS option that is plug-and-play in ArduPilot, and GUESSING multipath on a steel gas holder surrounded by other assets will be severe. We would not stake the project on it.

The one thing the Cube would genuinely have given us

A good gyroscope, temperature-stabilised and vibration-isolated, plus ArduPilot’s mature logging stack. That capability is real and we do want it — here is why:

An accelerometer measures specific force, not gravity. Any sideways acceleration — including the one produced by our own steering correction — appears as a phantom heading error of atan(alat / g). A modest 0.1 m/s² of sideways jerk creates a 0.58° lie. That is a feedback path running from the actuator straight back into the sensor, and it is why accelerometer-only heading loops on walls either oscillate or have to be detuned into uselessness. CERTAIN

The fix is a gyro whose sensitive axis lies along the surface normal, blended with the inclinometer: 

phi_hat += gyro_n * dt                          # fast, immune to linear acceleration
phi_hat += (phi_accel - phi_hat) * (dt / TAU)   # slow, absolute, drift-free

With TAU = 2 s and a 10 °/hr bias-stability gyro, the steady error contributed by gyro drift is 10/3600 × 2 = 0.0056° — negligible. We get an acceleration-immune heading and a clean rate signal for the derivative term.

An ICM-42688-P on the Teensy’s spare SPI chip-select does exactly this, with lower latency than a MAVLink hop to a Cube, for ₹1,999.

If the team still wants to buy a Cube

It is not useless — it is just mis-specified for this job. The only defensible way to use one would be:

  • Run it in MANUAL as a raw sensor and data logger only: stream RAW_IMU and ATTITUDE_QUATERNION to the Pi over MAVLink.
  • Do all control on the Teensy / Pi, exactly as described in Tab 4.
  • Never enable an ArduRover navigation mode, because none of them can represent our heading.

In that configuration we have bought a ₹51,999 IMU and logger. It is a defensible purchase for hardware quality and triple redundancy. It is not a fix for the straight-line problem, and it should not be presented as one.

Phase 0 — Measure first. Half a day. No code changes.

This is the highest-value half-day available to us

We currently cannot answer “does the rover bow consistently to one side, or wander randomly?” That single observation tells us whether the dominant cause is a bias (Findings 2, 3, 5) or the sign defect (Finding 1). Do this before touching any code.

  1. Axis identification. Flash the new firmware, send the D command, park the rover on the wall, and rotate it about 10° each way by hand about the surface normal. Record which sensor axis gives a signed, linear ±10° reading — that is the lateral axis. Confirm the axis we currently use gives the same number for both directions.
  2. Static noise and warm-up drift. Log the computed heading, stationary on the wall, for 30 minutes from cold start. This gives the real sensor bias and its temperature drift.
  3. Motion corruption. Log the heading while driving up at painting speed. The difference from the static trace tells us how much gyro we need. LIKELY we will see 0.3–1.5° of accelerometer noise from wheel-magnet cogging and pump vibration.
  4. Ground truth. Chalk-line one full pass and measure the deviation at every 1 m. Consistent bow, or random wander?

Also worth revisiting: the crab angle itself

We currently step sideways at about 30°. That means driving step / sin 30° = 2× the sideways distance, and losing 1.73 × step of height in the process — 260 mm of height lost for a 150 mm sideways step. At 90° (driving horizontally around the tank) the step distance equals the commanded distance exactly, encoder error is not amplified, and no height is lost at all. Because the turn is now closed on an absolute gravity reference, turn error does not accumulate from pass to pass, so the extra turn is cheap.

  • Move CSV logging out of the sensor-reading thread into a queue.Queue + writer thread. Today an SD-card flush() at line 241 blocks the heading update.
  • Drain the serial buffer to the newest line each cycle: while ser.in_waiting > 64: line = ser.readline(). readline() returns the oldest line, so a stall silently feeds us stale data.
  • Raise the Roboclaw baud rate from 38400 to 230400 in BasicMicro Motion Studio.
  • The new firmware timestamps every packet, so the Pi can now measure link latency instead of us guessing at it. It is logged as link_latency_ms.

Phase 3 — Fix the manoeuvre, not just the controller

What heading control cannot do

Perfect heading control gives a straight line, but not necessarily the right straight line. It cannot correct a sideways offset that already exists, and it cannot see pure sideways slip (the rover sliding without rotating). Wheel encoders cannot see that slip either. CERTAIN

Architecture note for the longer term

Today the control loop crosses three hops and a non-real-time operating system: 

SCL3300 → SPI → Teensy → USB → RPi 5 (Linux, Python, GIL) → UART 38400 → 2× Roboclaw

LIKELY the inner heading loop should move onto the Teensy. It already owns the sensor, it is hard real-time, and it can run the loop at 200 Hz deterministically. The Pi would then handle supervision: mission sequencing, lane counting, logging, the operator interface and the FlySky link.

That means moving the Roboclaw serial link from the Pi’s GPIO14/15 to a Teensy hardware serial port — a wiring change on the existing schematic, not a redesign. The Teensy 4.1 has eight hardware serial ports and we currently use one. Revisit this only if Phase 1 plus the latency housekeeping does not settle the loop.

These do not change the conclusions in Tabs 2 and 3 — those rest on our own source code and manufacturer datasheets. They change the numbers: the acceptance criterion, the error budget, and which Phase 3/4 option is worth funding.

A. Measurements we must take — nobody can answer these from a desk

#QuestionWhy it mattersWhat changes with the answerOwner
A1 Chalk-line one full pass and measure deviation every 1 m. Is the error a consistent bow to one side, or a random wander? Does it repeat pass to pass? This is the single most informative measurement available and it takes 20 minutes. A consistent bow points at a bias (Findings 2, 3, 5). A random wander points at the sign defect (Finding 1) or wheel slip. Re-orders the entire fix priority list. Highest priority item in this document. Rover team
A2 Which physical sensor axis points up the wall, and which points out of it? (Run the D diagnostic and rotate the rover ±10° by hand.) Determines whether the axis we currently steer on is the unsigned one. Two minutes of work. Confirms or refutes Finding 1 on the actual machine. Rover team
A3 Log the heading stationary on the wall for 30 minutes from cold start. Gives the real sensor bias and its warm-up drift. The datasheet allows up to 0.573°; the real figure is probably much smaller but we do not know it. Sets how often the Z calibration must be repeated during a shift. Rover team
A4 Log the heading while driving up at painting speed, with the pump running. The difference from A3 is the vibration and acceleration corruption of the accelerometer. Decides whether Phase 2 (the gyro) is mandatory or optional. Rover team
A5 Is the Roboclaw velocity PID actually tuned? What are the configured QPPS values per channel, and were the PID constants auto-tuned in BasicMicro Motion Studio? The code calls SpeedM1M2, which is velocity-PID mode — but with a wrong QPPS the loop saturates and behaves as if open-loop. If untuned, this is a sixth defect and a cheap fix. Rover team

C. Design questions the team must decide

#QuestionWhy we are askingOur recommendation
C1 Is there an umbilical — paint hose, air line, power tether? What does it weigh and how is it managed? LIKELY a top-three disturbance source, and it is almost never instrumented. Its pull grows with height and changes direction on every index move. This is exactly the constant disturbance that a P-only controller cannot cancel (Finding 5). Answer this before tuning gains. Consider a tether-management arm or a counterweight.
C2 Is the spray gun on the rover centreline? An off-centre gun produces a constant yaw torque proportional to spray pressure — another disturbance the current controller structurally cannot remove. Centre it if possible; otherwise the integral term must absorb it.
C3 Should the sideways index move be done at 30° (today) or 90°? At 30° we lose 260 mm of height for every 150 mm of sideways step, and encoder error is amplified 2×. At 90° neither happens. 90°, now that the turn is closed on an absolute reference.
C4 Do we accept a mechanical guide (taut wire) as a fallback if Phases 1–2 do not meet the coating spec? It is the most reliable option and standard on commercial tank crawlers, but it constrains how the rover is deployed. Keep it on the table. Decide after Phase 1 measurement, not before.
C5 Is the ₹1,999 gyro approved for Phase 2? It is the only hardware purchase we are recommending, and it removes the last known source of loop oscillation. Approve. It is 3.8% of the Cube Orange cost and addresses a mechanism we can quantify.
C6 Safety: can we add a hardware E-stop that cuts motor power independently of the Raspberry Pi? Today every emergency-stop path in Straight mode_testing2.py runs through Python on a non-real-time OS, on a rover carrying 240 VAC up a gas holder. Yes, regardless of anything else in this report.

What we are NOT waiting on

None of the above blocks Phase 1. The four critical defects are proven from our own source code and the Murata datasheet, and the patches are written. The measurements in section A should run in parallel with the code changes, not before them — except A1 and A2, which are worth the 20 minutes it takes to run them first.
